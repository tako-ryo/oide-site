Choosing a school from abroad is hard.

Most school websites are in Japanese, and the brochures all say the same things: small classes, friendly teachers, JLPT preparation (the Japanese Language Proficiency Test, from N5 for beginners to N1 for advanced). From the outside, you can't tell which course would actually suit you.

That's the part a teacher can help with. I'll ask what you can say in Japanese right now, what you want to be able to do, and how long you have. Then I'll tell you which schools match, and which don't.

I teach Japanese online to English speakers, and I run @helpmekansai, an Instagram account about the difference between standard Japanese and the Kansai dialect (the everyday Japanese of Osaka, Kyoto, Kobe and the wider Kansai region, spoken by around 20 million people). Most readers live outside Japan, and many have asked me the question you might have: "Where should I study?"

Is this for you?

Three situations I hear most often. You don't have to fit neatly into one.

1 to 3 months

"I want to try living in Japan first."

A short course while you travel. For many nationalities no visa is needed for stays up to 90 days. Good for testing whether you like a city, a school, and daily life in Japanese before committing to a year.

About 1 year

"I want to get properly good at Japanese."

A long-term course on a student visa. Full-time classes, usually 4 start dates a year (January, April, July and October). The school applies for your visa paperwork, and I help you prepare for it. This is the route if you're aiming for JLPT N2 (upper-intermediate) or work in Japan.

Not sure yet

"I don't know what's realistic."

That's fine. Tell me what you can say in Japanese now and what you'd like to be able to do. We work backwards from there. Sometimes the honest answer is "study online for six more months, then come."

What I check before a school goes on your list

Every school says "small classes and friendly teachers". These are the things I actually look at.

  1. Class size and level splitHow many levels the school runs, so you're not stuck in a class that's too easy or too hard for you.
  2. Who's in the roomThe nationality mix, and how many classmates you could fall back on English with. Fewer is better for your Japanese.
  3. Immigration standingWhether the school has the immigration bureau's "properly managed school" status (適正校, tekisei-kō). It makes your visa paperwork lighter.
  4. The real totalTuition plus enrolment, materials, insurance and housing fees, in one number. Brochures rarely show it that way.
  5. Start dates and deadlinesMost schools have 4 intakes a year, and the application deadline is months earlier. I'll tell you which one is realistic for you.
  6. English-speaking staffSomeone at the school who can help you in English on a bad day. Not every school has this.
  7. HousingDormitory, share house (a shared apartment with private rooms) or homestay, how far from school, and what it really costs.
  8. Where students go nextJLPT results, university places and jobs, where the school publishes them.

Visa and timing

The basics, so you know what to expect. The school handles the official paperwork.

Up to 90 days: usually no visa

Citizens of Canada, the US, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, and most of Europe can enter Japan as a temporary visitor for up to 90 days without applying for a visa in advance. You can take a short language course on that status. You can't work.

When to start: 1 to 2 months before you want to arrive is usually enough. Popular months fill up, so earlier is better.

Longer than 90 days: student visa

You need a student visa. The school applies for a Certificate of Eligibility (a document from Japanese immigration that says you can be issued a student visa) on your behalf, then you get the visa at a Japanese embassy or consulate in your country. You'll need to show that you or your family can cover tuition and living costs. With permission, students can work part-time up to 28 hours a week.

When to start: 6 to 9 months before the start date you want. Most schools start in January, April, July and October, and applications close a few months earlier.

One thing I don't do: I don't prepare or write your financial documents. You and your family prepare them, and they go to the school as they are. This keeps the process honest for you, for the school, and for me.

Questions people ask me

Is it really free?

Yes. You don't pay me anything at any point. When a student I introduced enrols, the school pays me a referral fee. That fee comes out of the school's marketing budget, not out of your tuition. If you ever feel a cost is unclear, ask me directly and I'll show you exactly who pays what.

Do you only work with certain schools?

No. I have referral arrangements with some schools and none with others. If the right school for you is one that doesn't pay me, I'll tell you, and you can apply to them directly. I'd rather you end up in the right place than in a school that pays me.

You're one person. What if you're busy?

Fair question. I keep the number of students I work with at any one time small, and you'll always have a direct way to reach me. If I can't do something well, I'll say so rather than let you wait.

I'm already talking to another agency. Can I still ask you?

Yes. A second opinion costs nothing. I won't contact any school on your behalf unless you ask me to.

Do I need to speak Japanese already?

No. Schools have beginner classes, and our conversations are in English. If you already know some Japanese, tell me roughly what you can do and we'll find a class at the right level rather than starting you from zero.

What if you suggest a school and I don't like it?

Then we look at others. The shortlist is a starting point, not a decision. You can also visit or video-call the school before applying. Nothing is committed until you send an application yourself.

Can I work while I study?

On a student visa, yes, up to 28 hours a week once you get work permission after arriving. On a 90-day temporary visitor stay, no. Many students on the long course do part-time work in cafés, convenience stores or hotels.

Do you help with housing?

I'll tell you what each school offers (dormitory, share house, homestay) and what the neighbourhood is like. Housing contracts are between you and the school or the housing company. I can help you understand the paperwork in English.

Are you an official agent?

I'm an independent Japanese teacher based in Osaka, working on my own under the name OIDE. Introductions to schools are referral arrangements. I'm not an immigration lawyer or a visa agency, so for visa questions beyond the basics I'll point you to the school or the official source.
